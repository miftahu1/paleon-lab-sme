Preparing your business for cloud migration

Moving to the cloud offers genuine benefits for many businesses—flexibility, scalability, and often lower costs. But successful cloud adoption requires careful planning.

Before you begin

The most important question isn't "should we move to the cloud?" It's "which of our workloads would benefit from cloud deployment?"

Not every application or service belongs in the cloud. Some workloads have specific requirements—regulatory, performance, or cost—that make on-premises deployment more sensible.

Understanding your current state

Start by documenting what you're running today:

  • What applications and services do you operate?
  • What infrastructure do they run on?
  • What are the dependencies between systems?
  • What are your current operating costs?
  • What regulatory or compliance requirements apply?

Identifying good candidates for migration

Good initial candidates for cloud migration typically have some of these characteristics:

  • Variable demand (the workload scales up and down)
  • Geographic distribution requirements
  • Development and testing environments
  • Web applications with internet-facing users
  • Workloads that aren't latency-sensitive

Poor initial candidates often include:

  • Applications with very high data transfer requirements
  • Systems tightly coupled to on-premises infrastructure
  • Workloads with specific hardware requirements
  • Applications that would require extensive rearchitecting

Choosing a migration strategy

Common approaches include:

Rehost ("lift and shift") — Move applications with minimal changes. Fastest approach but doesn't take full advantage of cloud capabilities.

Replatform — Make targeted optimisations during migration (e.g., move to managed database service). Moderate effort, moderate benefit.

Refactor — Rearchitect applications to be cloud-native. Highest effort but maximum long-term benefit.

Most businesses use a combination—rehosting some workloads for speed while refactoring others where the investment makes sense.

Planning your migration

A typical phased approach:

  1. Start with non-production environments to learn and prove your approach
  2. Migrate a non-critical but representative application
  3. Document what you learned and refine your process
  4. Move progressively more important workloads
  5. Keep reviewing costs and optimising as you go

Common pitfalls to avoid

  • Underestimating data transfer costs and times
  • Failing to implement proper cost monitoring from day one
  • Neglecting to train your team on cloud operations
  • Moving everything at once rather than in phases
  • Ignoring security and compliance until after migration

Getting started

If you're considering cloud migration, start with a structured assessment. Understand your current state, identify good candidates, and plan a phased approach that proves value at each step.

Replacing legacy technology without disrupting operations

Legacy systems are a common challenge. They work, but they're difficult to maintain, expensive to support, and hold your business back. Yet replacing them feels risky.

Why legacy replacement projects fail

Most legacy replacement failures share common causes:

  • Underestimating what the legacy system actually does
  • Attempting "big bang" replacements rather than phased transitions
  • Inadequate testing and validation
  • Poor data migration planning
  • Insufficient user training and change management

A better approach

Understand before you replace. Document what your legacy system does, not just what you think it should do. Interview users. Watch how people actually use it. Legacy systems often contain undocumented business logic that's critical to operations.

Plan for coexistence. Design your replacement so old and new systems can run side-by-side during transition. This lets you validate the new system thoroughly before cutting over completely.

Data migration is a project in itself. Don't underestimate it. Plan for data cleansing, validation, and testing. Run parallel operations to verify the migrated data is correct.

Phase the replacement. Replace components incrementally where possible. Prove each phase works before moving to the next. This contains risk and allows you to course-correct.

Managing the transition

The transition period is critical. You need:

  • Clear rollback procedures if problems arise
  • Monitoring to catch issues quickly
  • Support resources available during cutover
  • Communication plan to keep users informed
  • Realistic timeline that doesn't rush critical steps

The human element

Technology replacement is as much about people as systems. Users who've worked with legacy systems for years need proper training and support. Involve them early, listen to their concerns, and plan for an adjustment period.

Making the case for replacement

When building the business case, consider:

  • Ongoing maintenance and support costs
  • Risk of system failure
  • Opportunity cost (what you can't do because of legacy constraints)
  • Staff time spent working around limitations

The cost of replacing legacy systems is often less than the hidden cost of keeping them.

Improving operational efficiency through technology

Many businesses have operational inefficiencies that technology could solve—but finding and fixing them requires knowing where to look.

Identifying efficiency opportunities

Start by observing how work actually happens in your business:

  • What tasks do people repeat daily or weekly?
  • Where do people manually move data between systems?
  • What reports or summaries are created manually?
  • Where do approval workflows create bottlenecks?
  • What questions require digging through multiple systems to answer?

Common efficiency improvements

Automation — Repetitive tasks are prime candidates. If someone does the same thing every day, there's probably a way to automate it. Focus on high-frequency, low-variation tasks first.

Integration — When people manually move data between systems, integration can eliminate that work. Modern integration tools make connecting systems much easier than it used to be.

Process digitisation — Manual, paper-based, or spreadsheet-driven processes often benefit from proper software. The key is choosing tools that match how people actually work, not forcing people to adapt to rigid software.

Self-service capabilities — If staff spend time answering routine questions or requests, consider self-service options. Customer portals, knowledge bases, and automated systems can handle common requests.

Prioritising improvements

Not all efficiency improvements are worth pursuing. Prioritise based on:

  • Frequency — How often does this inefficiency occur?
  • Impact — How much time or money does it cost?
  • Implementation effort — How difficult is the solution?
  • Risk — What could go wrong if you change this?

Quick wins (high impact, low effort) should come first. They build momentum and demonstrate value.

Implementation approaches

When implementing efficiency improvements:

  • Start small — Prove value before scaling
  • Involve the people doing the work — They know the details
  • Measure before and after — Quantify the improvement
  • Plan for learning curve — New systems take time to become efficient
  • Iterate — First version doesn't need to be perfect

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Automating broken processes — Fix the process first, then automate
  • Over-engineering solutions — Simple improvements often work better
  • Ignoring the human element — Technology alone doesn't create efficiency
  • Failing to measure — If you don't measure, you can't improve

Long-term thinking

Efficiency improvement isn't a one-time project. Build a culture of continuous improvement where people feel empowered to suggest better ways of working.

Planning Guide October 2025

Planning a technology refresh

Technology infrastructure doesn't last forever. Planning when and how to refresh it helps avoid costly emergencies and supports business growth.

When to refresh

Consider a technology refresh when:

  • Hardware is reaching end-of-life
  • Software is no longer supported
  • Performance problems are affecting operations
  • Security vulnerabilities can't be patched
  • Maintenance costs are increasing significantly
  • You're planning business expansion

Proactive refreshes are almost always cheaper and less disruptive than reactive emergency replacements.

Planning your refresh

Assess your current state — Document what you have, how old it is, what it costs to maintain, and what problems exist.

Understand your requirements — What do you need to support today? What will you need in 2-3 years? Where are you growing?

Evaluate options — Consider both on-premises and cloud options. Sometimes a refresh is the right time to move to cloud services.

Plan for data migration — If you're replacing storage or database systems, data migration can be complex. Plan it carefully.

Budget realistically — Include hardware/software costs, implementation services, training, and contingency for unexpected issues.

Phasing the refresh

For larger environments, phase the refresh:

  1. Replace non-critical systems first to validate the approach
  2. Move to progressively more critical systems
  3. Keep old infrastructure running until new is proven
  4. Plan rollback options if problems arise

Minimising disruption

  • Schedule changes during low-activity periods
  • Communicate clearly with affected users
  • Test thoroughly before cutting over
  • Have support resources available during transition
  • Keep old systems available as fallback initially

Common refresh mistakes

  • Waiting until emergency replacement is necessary
  • Underestimating complexity of migration
  • Over-specifying capacity you won't use
  • Under-specifying capacity for growth
  • Neglecting training for new systems

Lifecycle planning

Build a technology lifecycle plan that identifies when different components should be reviewed for refresh. This turns infrastructure refresh from periodic crisis to planned ongoing process.

Typical refresh cycles:

  • User devices: 3-4 years
  • Servers: 4-5 years
  • Networking equipment: 5-7 years
  • Storage systems: 4-5 years

These are guidelines—actual cycles depend on your specific usage, requirements, and budget.
